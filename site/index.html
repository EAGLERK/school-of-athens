The School of Athens & The Death of Socrates

I · The stanza

A Renaissance stanza

Rome, 1508

Pope Julius II brings a twenty-five-year-old Raphael to Rome to paint his private rooms. The first, the Stanza della Segnatura, will take him until 1511.

Each wall gets one branch of knowledge: Theology, Poetry, Justice. The fourth is Philosophy, and Raphael fills it with more than fifty thinkers who never shared a room.

Raphael · 1509–1511 · fresco

The School of Athens

Plato and Aristotle

Plato points up, to the world of ideas, his Timaeus under his arm. Aristotle holds his Ethics and turns his palm to the earth. Raphael is often said to have given Plato the face of Leonardo da Vinci.

The painted halls

Every line of the floor and the coffered vaults runs to a point between the two men. Raphael built the hall from Bramante's plans for the new St Peter's, a church that would take another century to finish.

Athens, 399 BC

Almost two thousand years earlier

Before Plato wrote a word, his teacher was put on trial. Socrates, seventy years old, was convicted of impiety and of corrupting the young, and sentenced to die by hemlock.

Jacques-Louis David · 1787 · oil on canvas

The Death of Socrates

Still teaching

His friends had arranged an escape. He refused. David has him reach for the cup without looking at it, one finger raised, still arguing for the immortality of the soul.

“Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it and do not neglect it.”

His last words, in Plato's Phaedo

Plato, at the foot of the bed

David seats him there, old and turned away. In the Phaedo Plato records that he was ill that day and not present. He was under thirty.

On the stair, his wife Xanthippe is led away. The jailer who hands over the hemlock cannot look.

“The unexamined life is not worth living.”

Socrates at his trial · Plato, Apology 38a

Pope Julius II brought Raphael to Rome in 1508 to decorate his private apartments. The Stanza della Segnatura, probably meant as Julius's library, was the first room he painted. Its walls stand for Theology (the Disputa), Poetry (Parnassus), Justice, and Philosophy: this wall.

Plato, pointing upward with his Timaeus, and Aristotle, palm toward the earth with his Ethics, walk out of the vanishing point together. Plato is commonly read as a portrait of Leonardo da Vinci. Euclid, bending over a slate with compasses at lower right, is said to be the architect Bramante; Raphael put himself at the far right edge, looking out.

Heraclitus, brooding on the steps with his elbow on a block, does not appear in Raphael's full-size cartoon, now in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan. He was added late, and is often taken as a portrait of Michelangelo, who was painting the Sistine Chapel ceiling a short walk away.

The architecture follows Bramante's designs for the new St Peter's: coffered barrel vaults, a dome, statues of Apollo and Athena in the niches. The title came later; Raphael never called it The School of Athens.

In 399 BC an Athenian jury convicted Socrates of impiety and of corrupting the young. He refused to flee and drank hemlock in prison. David takes the scene from Plato's Phaedo, which records Socrates' last conversation, about the immortality of the soul.

The picture was commissioned by Charles-Michel Trudaine de la Sablière and shown at the Paris Salon of 1787, two years before the Revolution. Sir Joshua Reynolds called it the greatest effort of art since the Sistine Chapel and the Stanze of Raphael.

David changes the record where it suits him. Socrates reaches for the cup while still talking. Plato, who says in the Phaedo that he was ill and absent, sits at the foot of the bed as an old man, though he was under thirty. Crito holds his teacher's thigh; Xanthippe, sent away earlier in the text, climbs the stair in the background.

The Met acquired it in 1931.
